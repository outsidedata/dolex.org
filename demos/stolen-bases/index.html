The Rise and Fall of the Stolen Base

How baseball's most exciting play went from 200 steals per team to 40, then roared back with Rickey Henderson.

I want to explore stolen bases in baseball history. How has base stealing changed over time?

This is fascinating. You can see three distinct eras:
In the early 1900s, teams were stealing over 200 bases per season. Then something dramatic happened around 1920 - the stolen base nearly died. By 1950, teams were down to just 40 steals per year. That's Babe Ruth's legacy - when home runs became king, why risk getting thrown out? Then in the 1960s and 70s, it started climbing again. By the late 1980s, teams were back to 130+ steals. But look at the 2000s - it's declining again as analytics shows most stolen base attempts aren't worth the risk.

What about single-season records? Who had the best individual seasons?
Let me filter to the modern era (1920+) when caught stealing was actually tracked. The pre-1920 numbers are incomplete.
Rickey Henderson's 130 steals in 1982 is the modern record. What's remarkable is how he dominates this list - five of the top 15 seasons belong to him. Vince Coleman has three 100+ steal seasons, all consecutive (1985-87). Lou Brock's 118 in 1974 was the record until Henderson shattered it. Maury Wills's 104 in 1962 is historically significant - it was the first 100-steal season since 1915 and kicked off the stolen base revival.
Has the success rate changed? Are runners getting smarter about when to steal?
This is where it gets interesting. Let me calculate the success rate (steals / attempts) over time.
The story here is about selection pressure. In 1920, runners were getting caught nearly half the time - a 52% success rate. Catchers were throwing out everyone who tried. By the 1930s, only the best runners were attempting steals, pushing success rates to 74%. Then the 1950s show a collapse back to 55% - a generation of players who forgot how to steal. The modern era (1980s-2010s) shows teams getting smarter. Success rates climbed to 74% by 2012 as analytics identified which situations actually favor the stolen base. Fewer attempts, but higher quality.
Let's look at the 1980s specifically. How did Henderson, Coleman, and Tim Raines compare head-to-head?
The 1980s were the golden age of speed. Let me chart all three against each other.
Three different styles of excellence: Henderson (blue) was volatile but dominant. His 130 in 1982 towers over everyone. He'd have down years (56 in 1981, 41 in 1987) but always bounced back. Coleman (green) burst onto the scene in 1985 with 110 steals as a rookie - then put up 107 and 109 the next two years. Three straight 100+ seasons, but injuries derailed him by 1989. Raines (orange) was the steady hand. Never cracked 90 steals, but maintained a consistent 50-75 range for over a decade. His career success rate of 84.7% was the best of the three. Henderson outlasted them all, playing until 2003. The last man standing from baseball's speed era.
